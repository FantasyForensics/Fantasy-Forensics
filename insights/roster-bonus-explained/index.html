Why 9,973 Doesn't Beat 9,985: The Value Adjustment, Explained

Brown plus Collins sums to 9,973; Gibbs alone is 9,985. Raw math calls it a coin flip. Our calculator calls it for Gibbs by 6,631 points — this is the value adjustment doing that, with the math shown.

Here is a trade that looks like a coin flip. One side gives Jahmyr Gibbs, priced 9,985 — the RB1 on our board. The other gives Chase Brown (4,611) and Nico Collins (5,362), which sum to 9,973. Raw totals: Gibbs by 12 points, a rounding error. Our calculator doesn't call it even. It scores the deal clearly for the Gibbs side — because of the value adjustment, the adjustment applied to every verdict the calculator renders.

Roster spots are the currency

The logic is roster math. This league starts eleven players a week. Gibbs occupies one slot and produces like it: 30.7 points per game through Week 5, a 77% snap share, the No. 2 usage rank among all running backs, receivers and tight ends. Brown and Collins occupy two slots — and the second slot is never free, because Collins' points come instead of your next bench option's, not in addition to them. So the calculator adds a value adjustment to the side whose production is concentrated into fewer spots: the best player in a deal carries value beyond his raw price, and the adjustment credits that side the amount that balances that difference. Gibbs' side takes back one player instead of two, collects a 6,619-point value adjustment, and lands at 16,604. Brown and Collins collect none and stay at 9,973. The 6,631-point gap is what that concentration is actually worth in this deal.

It tempers deals; it doesn't crown studs

The adjustment cuts both ways, deliberately. It follows concentration, not headcount — an even trade can earn one when one side is top-heavy — and a true 1-for-1 earns none at all — one player a side, there is no concentration to price, so the raw difference is the whole story. Nor does it hand the win to the single biggest name: two genuinely strong starters still beat one great player whenever their combined value clears his price plus the adjustment. Picks count as assets the same way, which is why four late 2nds don't add up to one early 1st in our calculator even when the raw sums insist they should. The raw totals never disappear, either: every verdict leads with the adjusted Trade value and prints the Raw sum right beneath it, with the value adjustment on its own line whenever one applies — never a hidden thumb on the scale.
